A language model does arithmetic on numbers. Text has to become a sequence of integers before the model sees it, and the integers have to become text again on the way out. The component that does both is the tokenizer. It is trained separately from the model and sits in front of it.

Take a real ticket from the Time Travel Inc. queue:

Card payments failing at 3-D Secure step

Every character is already stored as bytes. In UTF-8 this sentence is 40 bytes, each a number from 0 to 255:

C   a   r   d   ␣   p   a   y   m   e   n   t   s  ...
67  97  114 100 32  112 97  121 109 101 110 116 115 ...

That gives three obvious ways to cut text into pieces, and each has a cost.

Cut byVocabularyThe sentence aboveWhat goes wrong
Byte25640 tokensSequences are long. The model's context window is counted in tokens, so you fit less in, and it must learn spelling from scratch.
Wordhundreds of thousands7 tokensAny word not seen in training has no number at all: a new supplier name, INC0010450, a typo.
Sub-wordyou choose, often 30,000 to 100,000about 9 tokensA middle path: common words are one token, rare ones are spelled from pieces. Nothing is ever out of vocabulary.

Formally, a tokenizer is a pair of functions over a vocabulary V of size |V|:

encode : text → (t1, …, tn), ti ∈ {0, …, |V| − 1} decode : (t1, …, tn) → text

with the requirement that decode(encode(x)) = x for every string x. Starting from bytes guarantees that requirement, because any text at all is a sequence of bytes. The question for the next step is how to choose the larger pieces.
