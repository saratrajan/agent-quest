Tokens are the unit everything else in this course is measured in.

  • Context window. A model reads at most a fixed number of tokens at once. The ticket you are triaging, the past incidents you retrieve for it, the runbook and the model's own answer all share that budget.
  • Cost and speed. Hosted models charge per token in and per token out, and a model generates one token at a time. Twice the tokens is roughly twice the bill and twice the wait.
  • What the model can see. The model never sees letters, only token numbers. Whatever the tokenizer splits awkwardly, the model has to reassemble.

That last point matters for an on-call agent, because tickets are full of text that no tokenizer was trained on:

TextBytesTokens in a production tokenizerWhy
payment71A common word has earned its own token.
INC0010450104An identifier is rare, so it is spelled from fragments such as "INC", "001", "045", "0".
ERR_CONN_RESET143It splits at the underscores into "ERR", "_CONN" and "_RESET"; the model has to treat three pieces as one name.

Two consequences you will meet again in the retrieval sessions:

  • A model compares incident numbers and error codes less reliably than it compares ordinary words. Exact identifiers are better matched with plain keyword search than by meaning.
  • The tokenizer trained on your own incidents learns your vocabulary. In the experiments you will see it give "supplier" or "checkout" a single token long before a general-purpose tokenizer would have, and still lose to it on ordinary English.

Production tokenizers add refinements to what you are about to write: they split text into words and punctuation before merging so that merges never cross a word boundary, and they reserve special tokens for marking the start and end of messages. The core loop is the one you are about to build.
