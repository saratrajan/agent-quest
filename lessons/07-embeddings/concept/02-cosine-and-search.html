Two vectors are alike when they point the same way. The standard measure is cosine similarity: the cosine of the angle between them.

cos(a, b) = (a · b) / (‖a‖ ‖b‖)

The top is the dot product, multiplying matching numbers and adding up. The bottom is the two lengths multiplied, where a length is the square root of the sum of squares. The result runs from 1 (same direction) through 0 (unrelated) to −1 (opposite).

[1, 0][1, 1]11 and √21 ÷ 1.414 = 0.7071
[3, 4][4, 3]12 + 12 = 245 and 524 ÷ 25 = 0.96
[1, 0][0, 5]01 and 50

Dividing by the lengths is what makes it a measure of direction only. Turn and stretch b below and watch which number responds to which slider.

That suggests a shortcut. If every vector is first scaled to length 1 (normalised), the bottom of the formula is 1 and the cosine is simply the dot product. And a dot product against many vectors at once is one matrix multiplication.

So search over a store of vectors is short. Put the stored vectors in a matrix, one per row. Then, for a query vector:

  1. normalise the rows of the matrix, and the query;
  2. multiply the matrix by the query, giving one cosine per stored vector;
  3. sort, and keep the top k.

The top two are rows 2 and 0. Your first tests check exactly these numbers.

Comparing against every stored vector is called exact or brute-force search. For 400 incidents of 768 numbers each it is about 300,000 multiplications, which takes well under a millisecond. It stays practical into the hundreds of thousands. Beyond that, stores use an approximate index that looks at a small fraction of the vectors and accepts occasionally missing a true neighbour in exchange for speed. That trade is what a vector database manages for you, and it is the subject of the next session. Today you write the exact version, so that you know precisely what the database is approximating.
