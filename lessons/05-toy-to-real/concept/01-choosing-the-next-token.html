A model does not output text. It outputs a score for every token, and a separate step picks one. That step, sampling, has three controls, and every model you will ever call exposes them. They change the output a great deal without touching the model.

Temperature divides the scores before the softmax. Below 1 it sharpens the distribution towards the favourite; above 1 it flattens it. On scores [2, 1, 0]:

TemperatureScores after dividingProbabilities
1.0[2, 1, 0]0.665, 0.245, 0.090
0.5[4, 2, 0]0.867, 0.117, 0.016
2.0[1, 0.5, 0]0.506, 0.307, 0.186

A temperature of 0 is a convention meaning "always take the most likely token". It is handled as a special case, since nothing can be divided by zero.

Top-k keeps the k most likely tokens and discards the rest. Top-p, also called nucleus sampling, keeps the smallest set of most-likely tokens whose probabilities add up to at least p. On probabilities [0.5, 0.3, 0.15, 0.05]:

SettingKeptWhy
top-k = 2first twothe two highest, whatever their probabilities
top-p = 0.75first two0.5 + 0.3 = 0.8 reaches 0.75
top-p = 0.85first three0.8 falls short, so the third is needed

Top-k cuts a fixed number; top-p adapts. When the model is sure, top-p keeps one or two tokens; when it is unsure, it keeps many. Both exist to remove the long tail of unlikely tokens, which is where nonsense comes from: each tail token is improbable, but together they are picked often enough to derail a long answer.

Try the three controls together on one distribution.

The order matters and is the one your code will use: temperature, then top-k, then top-p, then a random draw from what is left.

Source: Holtzman et al. (2020), The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration, which introduced nucleus sampling.
