Two practical matters remain, and then the measurements.

Chunking. An embedding is one vector for a whole text. For a short text that is ideal. For a long one, such as a full ticket thread with twenty work notes, the vector is an average of everything in it, and a query about one detail matches weakly. The remedy is to split long documents into chunks, embed each chunk, and return the document whose chunk matched best. Chunks overlap a little so that a sentence cut at a boundary survives whole in one of them. Twelve words in chunks of 5 with an overlap of 2:

ChunkWordsStarts at
1w1 w2 w3 w4 w5word 1
2w4 w5 w6 w7 w8word 4 (5 − 2 = 3 further on)
3w7 w8 w9 w10 w11word 7
4w10 w11 w12word 10; the text ends here

Chunk size is a trade. Small chunks match precisely and lose context; large chunks keep context and blur. There is no correct size, only one that measures best on your data.

The vector database. Your session 7 search held every vector in one matrix in memory. A vector database such as Chroma, adopted today in store.py, adds what a real system needs around that: the vectors persist on disk, each is stored with its text and metadata, results can be filtered by metadata during the search, and an approximate index (a graph in which each vector links to its near neighbours) lets it search millions of vectors by visiting a few hundred. The small disagreement is mostly ties: many incidents have identical text, and the two methods break ties differently.

Hybrid is fractionally best and costs nothing extra, so it is the sensible default. Re-ranking with a small general model took two minutes and made the ordering slightly worse. Chunking made no difference because the signal sits in the first two lines of a ticket. None of this means the techniques are useless; it means this data did not need them, and you only know that because you measured.

Three things stand out. Retrieval transformed the model: service from 66% to about 90%, priority from 3% to 54%. One retrieved incident was enough for service and nowhere near enough for priority, and ten were no better than five for nearly twice the tokens. And the model with evidence still lost to a plain vote on priority, until one added sentence told it how to use the evidence. That sentence also turned one of the two real P1s into a P3. Every row of this table is a design decision with a cost, and the last experiment asks you to make it.
